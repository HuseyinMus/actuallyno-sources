# Video 7: Does sugar make kids hyper?

Sources for the *Actually, No* Short **"Does sugar make kids hyper?"**. Each claim in the script is listed with its primary source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Abstracts were read from Europe PMC on 2026-10-26.

## 1. "A 1995 analysis pooled 23 experiments where no one knew who got sugar" (10–22 s)
- **Source:** Wolraich M. L., Wilson D. B. & White J. W., "The effect of sugar on behavior or cognition in children: a meta-analysis", *JAMA* 274(20), 1617–1621 (1995). doi:10.1001/jama.274.20.1617, PMID 7474248.
- **Study selection:** studies had to use "a placebo (artificial sweetener) condition" and "blind the subjects, parents, and research staff to the conditions".
- **Size:** "Sixteen reports met the inclusion criteria for a total of 23 within-subject design studies."
- **Result:** "the 95% confidence interval for all 14 mean effect sizes included 0".
- **Conclusion:** "sugar does not affect the behavior or cognitive performance of children. The strong belief of parents may be due to expectancy and common association. However, a small effect of sugar or effects on subsets of children cannot be ruled out."
- **Check:** "At all" overstates the paper, because the authors keep the caveat about a small effect or subsets. Narration: "no measurable effect"; on screen: "no effect found".
- **On screen:** `Wolraich, Wilson & White, JAMA 1995 · 23 blinded studies`

## 2. Supporting trial
- **Source:** Wolraich M. L. et al., "Effects of diets high in sucrose or aspartame on the behavior and cognitive performance of children", *N Engl J Med* 330(5), 301–307 (1994). PMID 8277950.
- **Design:** a "double-blind controlled trial" with "25 normal preschool children" and "23 school-age children … described by their parents as sensitive to sugar". Each child followed three diets for three weeks each.
- **Results:**
  - "For the children described as sugar-sensitive, there were no significant differences among the three diets in any of 39 behavioral and cognitive variables."
  - Preschoolers: "only 4 of the 31 measures differed significantly … no consistent pattern".
- **Conclusion:** "Even when intake exceeds typical dietary levels, neither dietary sucrose nor aspartame affects children's behavior or cognitive function."
- This trial is cited in the description and the sources file. It is not named in the narration.

## 3. The placebo twist (22–38 s)
- **Source:** Hoover D. W. & Milich R., "Effects of sugar ingestion expectancies on mother-child interactions", *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology* 22(4), 501–515 (1994). doi:10.1007/BF02168088, PMID 7963081.
- **Wording:**
  - "thirty-five 5- to 7-year-old boys reported by their mothers to be behaviorally 'sugar sensitive,' and their mothers, were randomly assigned to experimental and control groups"
  - "mothers were told their children had received a large dose of sugar … all children actually received the placebo (aspartame)"
  - "Mothers in the sugar expectancy condition rated their children as significantly more hyperactive."
  - "these mothers exercised more control by maintaining physical closeness, as well as showing trends to criticize, look at, and talk to their sons more"
- **Checks:**
  - (a) The criticism finding is only a **trend**, so the narration says "tended to criticize more".
  - (b) "Half the moms" is approximate: 35 pairs were randomly split into two groups, and the abstract does not give the group sizes. Narration: "one group of moms".
  - (c) The boys were picked because their mothers believed they were sugar-sensitive.
- **On screen:** `Hoover & Milich, J. Abnorm. Child Psychol. 1994`

## 4. "The word 'sugar' did everything" (38–45 s)
- **Check:** This is stronger than the evidence. The 1994 study shows that the expectation changed what mothers saw and how they acted, and the 1995 meta-analysis says belief "may be due to expectancy". Narration: "The word 'sugar' changed what the moms saw."
- **"For most kids":** this reflects the 1995 caveat that "effects on subsets of children cannot be ruled out".

## 5. Teeth (38–45 s)
- **Source:** WHO news release, "WHO calls on countries to reduce sugars intake among adults and children", 4 March 2015, on the *Guideline: Sugars intake for adults and children*. It links free sugars above 10% of energy to "higher rates of dental caries (commonly referred to as tooth decay)". <https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children>
- **Wording rule:** say "linked to tooth decay", not "causes".
- **On screen:** `WHO sugars guideline, 2015`

## 6. ADHD: less settled
The studies above test typical children and children that parents call "sugar-sensitive". They do not settle whether sugar matters for children with ADHD, and that research is mixed:
- Del-Ponte B. et al., "Sugar consumption and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD): A birth cohort study", *J Affect Disord* (2019), doi:10.1016/j.jad.2018.09.051. In 2,924 children from the Pelotas cohort, "no association between sucrose consumption between 6 and 11 years of age and incidence of ADHD".
- Farsad-Naeimi A. et al., "Sugar consumption, sugar sweetened beverages and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis", *Complement Ther Med* (2020), doi:10.1016/j.ctim.2020.102512. Across seven observational studies (25,945 people) it found "a positive relationship … however, there was heterogeneity", and "Future well-designed studies … are necessary".
- These are observational associations, and they point in different directions. The film does not discuss ADHD. This is why it says "for most kids".
