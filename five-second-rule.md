# Video 6: The five-second rule

Sources for the *Actually, No* Short **"The five-second rule"**. Each claim in the script is listed with its primary source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Checked 2026-10-26.

## 1. "Legend says Genghis Khan…" (3–10 s)
- **Source:** Paul Dawson & Brian Sheldon, *Did You Just Eat That? Two Scientists Explore Double-Dipping, the Five-Second Rule, and Other Food Myths in the Lab* (W. W. Norton, 2018), as quoted by Science Friday:
  - The book "traces the origins to legends around Genghis Khan".
  - The legend's wording: "If food fell on the floor, it could stay there as long as Khan allowed."
  - <https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/the-origin-of-the-five-second-rule>
- **Check:**
  - The primary-ish source gives **no "five hours"**; the rule is "as long as Khan allowed". "Five hours" appears only in secondary retellings (a sermon-illustration site, magazine pieces), and those disagree with each other.
  - The story is a legend, not documented history. The first known print mention of a floor-time rule is in a 1995 novel, and there it is a "twenty-second rule" (Wikipedia, "Five-second rule"; Food Republic).
- **Narration:** "legend says". No duration is attributed to Khan.
- **On screen:** `legend · Dawson & Sheldon, 2018`

## 2. The Rutgers study (10–26 s)
- **Source:** Miranda R. C. & Schaffner D. W., "Longer Contact Times Increase Cross-Contamination of *Enterobacter aerogenes* from Surfaces to Food", *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* 82(21), 6490–6496 (2016). doi:10.1128/AEM.01838-16. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5066366>
- **Design:**
  - 4 surfaces: stainless steel, tile, wood, carpet.
  - 4 foods: watermelon, bread, bread with butter, gummy candy.
  - 4 contact times: <1, 5, 30 and 300 s.
  - 2 inoculum matrices: tryptic soy broth and peptone buffer.
  - In all, 128 scenarios, each replicated 20 times, giving **2,560 measurements**.
- **Wording:** "Some transfer takes place 'instantaneously,' at times of <1 s, disproving the five-second rule."
- **Checks:**
  - (a) Only 2 of the 4 surfaces are floors. Narration: "four surfaces".
  - (b) The test bacterium is "a nonpathogenic, food-grade microorganism", chosen because it attaches like *Salmonella*.
  - (c) The script's "finally tested it" is not accurate. Earlier tests exist: "The first known research recorded on this topic was performed at the University of Illinois" (paper, introduction).
- **On screen:** `Miranda & Schaffner, Appl. Environ. Microbiol. 2016`

## 3. Watermelon, gummy candy, carpet (26–37 s)
- **Watermelon:** "More bacteria transferred to watermelon (∼0.2 to 97%) than to any other food"; "When watermelon is cut, it is very moist, and moisture is known to facilitate transfer"; "Transfer of bacteria from surfaces to food appears to be affected most by the moisture of the food."
- **Gummy candy:** "least bacteria transferred to gummy candy (∼0.1 to 62%)".
- **Carpet:** "Carpet transferred fewer bacteria than the other contact surfaces" (Results); "Carpet has very low transfer rates, compared with those of tile and stainless steel" (Discussion).
- **On screen:** `watermelon ~0.2–97% · gummy ~0.1–62%` and `carpet: lowest transfer`

## 4. "Time still matters" (37–45 s)
- **Source:** same paper: "longer contact times result in more transfer", with other factors "of equal or greater importance".
- **Check:** Not true in every combination. For watermelon with broth-inoculated surfaces, "there was no significant difference … at different contact times". Narration: "usually".
- **On screen:** a schematic curve labelled "schematic: more contact time → usually more transfer". It is not drawn from the paper's data points.

## 5. Health wording
- The study measured transfer of a harmless bacterium. It did not measure illness. The film doesn't say dropped food "makes you sick"; it says more bacteria transfer.

- "four floors" became "four surfaces".
- "the more bacteria" became "the more bacteria, usually".
