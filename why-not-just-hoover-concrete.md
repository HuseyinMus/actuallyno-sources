# Video 20: Why not just pour Hoover Dam in one go?

Sources for this *Why not just...?* Short on the *Actually, No* channel. Each claim is listed with its source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Sources read on 2026-11-12.

Main sources (the dam's owner and operator):
- **USBR essay**: U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, "Hoover Dam: Concrete" (history essays): https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/history/essays/concrete.html
- **USBR FAQ**: U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, "Hoover Dam: Frequently Asked Questions and Answers": https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/faqs/damfaqs.html

## 1. The easy idea: fill the canyon with concrete in one continuous pour
- Volume: the film says "over 3 million cubic yards" (the dam itself: 3.25 million). USBR FAQ: "There are 4,360,000 cubic yards of concrete in the dam, powerplant and appurtenant works." (The FAQ gives 3.25 million cubic yards for the dam itself.)
- **On screen:** `U.S. Bureau of Reclamation`

## 2. Fail 1: setting concrete makes heat, and it would crack
- USBR FAQ (question): "How was chemical heat caused by setting concrete in the dam dissipated?"
- USBR essay: the columns were built "to allow the tremendous heat produced by the curing concrete to dissipate"; without that, "The resulting stresses would have caused the dam to crack and crumble away."
- **Wording in the film:** "Here's the problem. Setting concrete makes its own heat. One giant block cools unevenly, and cracks."
- **On screen:** `USBR: stresses "would have caused the dam to crack"`

## 3. Fail 2: just wait for it to cool
- USBR essay: "Bureau of Reclamation engineers calculated that if the dam were built in a single continuous pour… it would have taken 125 years for the concrete to cool to ambient temperatures".
- **Wording in the film:** "Okay, so just wait for it to cool? Reclamation's engineers did the math. One pour: 125 years."
- **On screen:** `USBR: one pour = 125 years to cool`

## 4. The real solution: blocks, pipes, ice water, grout
- Blocks: USBR FAQ: "An estimated 215 blocks make up the dam." They "varied in size from about 60 feet square at the upstream face of the dam to about 25 feet square at the downstream face". USBR essay: "the columns rose in five foot lifts." USBR FAQ: "Concrete placement in any one block was limited to five feet in 72 hours." Interlocking: "Adjacent columns were locked together by a system of vertical keys on the radial joints".
- Pipes: USBR FAQ: "By embedding more than 582 miles of 1-inch steel pipe in the concrete". USBR essay: "river water was circulated through these pipes", then "chilled water from a refrigeration plant on the lower cofferdam was circulated through the coils to finish the cooling."
- Ice: USBR FAQ: the refrigeration plant "could produce 1,000 tons of ice in 24 hours."
- Glue: USBR FAQ: "After the concrete was cooled, a cement and water mixture called grout" was forced between the columns "to form a monolithic (one-piece) structure." USBR essay: grout bonded "the entire structure into a monolithic whole."
- **Wording in the film:** "about 215 separate blocks, 5 feet at a time." / "thin steel pipe, 1 inch wide. 582 miles of it." / "First, river water ran through. Then ice water, from a plant that could make 1,000 tons of ice a day." / "they pumped grout into the gaps, and the blocks locked into one piece."
- **On screen:** `USBR: ~215 blocks, 5-ft lifts`, `USBR: 582+ miles of 1-inch pipe`, `USBR: plant = 1,000 tons of ice / 24 h`, `USBR: grouted into one monolith`

## 5. The twist: 125 years, done in about two
- USBR FAQ: "The first concrete for the dam was placed on June 6, 1933, and the last on May 29, 1935." "Cooling was completed in March 1935." All features completed by March 1, 1936.
- **Wording in the film:** "The dam that would take 125 years to cool was poured in under two. June 1933 to May 1935." (June 6, 1933 to May 29, 1935 is just under two years.)
- **On screen:** `USBR: first pour Jun 6 1933, last May 29 1935`

## Checked and dropped
- "Hoover Dam's concrete is still curing today": widely repeated, but neither USBR page says it; not used.
- Pipe totals other than 582 miles (592, 662 in secondary sources): not used.
