## 1. The problem: a flat, swampy city barely above the lake
- Tribune, April 9, 1857, "A Higher Grade" (letter): "The present grade of Lake street is about ten feet above low water."
- Wikipedia summarising the 1854 Tribune and 1856 Putnam's Monthly accounts: drainage was inadequate, "resulting in large bodies of standing and pathogenic water", and the city suffered epidemics, including the 1854 cholera outbreak. Primary: Tribune, July 12, 1854, "The Streets and the Cholera Again".
- **Wording in the film:** "The city sat on a swamp, barely above the lake." Disease is said as "standing water was linked to cholera outbreaks" (health rule: "linked to", never "caused").
- **On screen:** `Chicago Tribune, 1854-57`, `Lake St "about ten feet above low water" · Tribune 1857`, `Tribune, July 1854: "The Streets and the Cholera"`

## 2. Why "dig deeper" fails: no slope to drain
- **On screen:** `Encyclopedia of Chicago: too flat to drain`
- Encyclopedia of Chicago (search snippet): the flat site made gravity drainage hard, so the grade had to be raised for underground sewers.
- Mechanism (same as drawn): a sewer drains by gravity; dug deeper under a street that is already only a few feet above the river/lake, the pipe would sit at or below the outlet, so the water has nowhere to go.
- **Wording in the film:** "So, dig deeper? Then the sewage has nowhere to go. A sewer needs a slope, and Chicago had no downhill left."

## 3. The flip: pipes laid on the street, then buried
- Chicago's Board of Sewerage Commissioners was created in 1855 and hired Ellis S. Chesbrough; his plan (1855–56) ran the sewers above the old street level and covered them with fill, raising the street grade.
- Wikipedia: workers "laid drains, covered and refinished roads and sidewalks with several feet of soil".
- **How high:** sources give a range. Press and Tribune, Jan 1, 1859, "City Improvements in 1858": business blocks raised to grade "from four to six feet". Press and Tribune, Feb 24, 1859: "each from four to six feet." Cincinnati Commercial reprinted in Press and Tribune, June 21, 1859: "The level of the city on an average is raised three or four feet." Encyclopedia of Chicago (snippet): South Side streets raised "an average of between four and five feet". The film says "several feet" and shows no number for the streets.
- **On screen:** `Chesbrough sewer plan, 1855-56`, then `Tribune 1859: blocks raised "four to six feet"`

## 4. Raising the buildings with jackscrews
- **First brick building, January 1858** (Randolph and Dearborn): Tribune, Jan 26, 1858: "The building was raised up bodily six feet and two inches," with "some two hundred screws and fifteen men". (The Weekly Chicago Times, Jan 21, 1858, gave 300 screw-jacks and 7 ft before the work: not used.)
- **Lake Street block, March 1860** (between Clark and LaSalle; a continuous brick row of shops and offices):
  - Press and Tribune, March 26, 1860, "On the Rise": "There will be some 600 men employed, and about 6,000 screws."
  - Press and Tribune, April 2, 1860, "The Great Building-Raising": "The block has been raised four feet eight inches, the required height, in five days"; "six hundred workmen have been employed, each man in charge of from eight to ten screws."
  - Same article: the block stayed "filled with occupants"; "The internal order of the block has prevailed undisturbed."
  - (The March 29 report gives 4 ft 10 in mid-job; the April 2 final figure, 4 ft 8 in, is used.)
- **Wording in the film:** "six hundred men turned six thousand jackscrews, a tiny bit at a time" (both numbers from the same contemporary reports).
- **On screen:** `Press and Tribune, Mar 26 & Apr 2, 1860`

## 5. The twist: the Tremont House rose while it stayed open (1861)
- Tribune, Jan 22, 1861, "The Tremont House Improvement": "Ely, Smith & Pullman, raisers." (George Pullman, later of the Pullman sleeping car.)
- Tribune, Jan 24, 1861: "it is proposed to keep the House open, and its business as usual".
- Tribune, Feb 26, 1861, "One, Two, Three, and Up She Goes!": "Five thousand screws"; "five hundred were alone employed to attend the screws"; "it must be lifted full six feet".
- Tribune, Feb 12, 1861: "business there abouts goes on as usual." Tribune, March 15, 1861: "The business of the Tremont goes on as usual."
- **Wording in the film:** "The next year, they lifted a giant brick hotel six feet. It never closed. Business as usual." The draft's "guests checked in, ate dinner" is more detailed than the verified quotes, so the film says only that the hotel stayed open and did business as usual.
- **On screen:** `Tremont House · Chicago Tribune, Jan-Mar 1861`

## Checked and dropped
- "Guests checked in, ate dinner… while the building rose under them" (draft): softened. The Tribune confirms the hotel stayed open with business as usual; a well-known account of guests not noticing (David Macrae, *The Americans at Home*, 1870) could not be read in the original, so it is not used.
- "Thousands of screw jacks": kept with the exact contemporary figures (6,000 for Lake Street, 5,000 for the Tremont).
- Cholera death toll (often given as "six percent of the city" for 1854): not used; the film only says outbreaks were linked to standing water.
- Street-raise height: no single number; sources range from 3–4 ft (average) to 4–6 ft (business blocks), so the film says "several feet".
