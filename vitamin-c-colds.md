# Video 9: Does vitamin C stop colds?

Sources for the *Actually, No* Short on vitamin C and the common cold. Each claim in the script is listed with its primary source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Checked 2026-10-29. The abstract was read from Europe PMC (PMID 23440782).

## 1. Linus Pauling: two Nobel Prizes
- **Source:** Nobel Prize API (api.nobelprize.org, laureate "Linus Pauling"):
  - 1954 Chemistry, unshared, "for his research into the nature of the chemical bond and its application to the elucidation of the structure of complex substances".
  - 1962 Peace, unshared, "for his fight against the nuclear arms race between East and West".
- **His claim:** Linus Pauling, *Vitamin C and the Common Cold* (W. H. Freeman, 1970). The book argued that large daily doses of vitamin C prevent colds and make them milder.
- **"Sales exploded" is not used.** We found no primary sales data, only popular retellings, so the film says the book "made the idea famous" and shows bottles as an illustration with no number.
- **Pauling is shown only as a stylized silhouette.** There is no photo and no likeness.
- **On screen:** `Nobel Prizes: Chemistry 1954 · Peace 1962`

## 2. The Cochrane review
- **Source:** Hemilä H. & Chalker E., "Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold", *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* 2013, Issue 1, CD000980. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4, PMID 23440782.
- **Scope:** placebo-controlled trials only, with at least 0.2 g a day of vitamin C. "The majority of included trials were randomised, double-blind trials."

### 2a. Taking it every day: incidence (12–23 s)
- **Wording:**
  - "Twenty-nine trial comparisons involving 11,306 participants contributed to the meta-analysis on the risk ratio (RR) of developing a cold whilst taking vitamin C regularly"
  - "In the general community trials involving 10,708 participants, the pooled RR was 0.97 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.94 to 1.00)."
  - Authors: "The failure of vitamin C supplementation to reduce the incidence of colds in the general population indicates that routine vitamin C supplementation is not justified".
- **Check:** "No fewer colds. None." is too absolute. The point estimate is 3% fewer, and the interval just reaches no difference. Narration: "about the same number of colds"; on screen: "RR 0.97: no meaningful drop".
- **On screen:** `Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane 2013 · 29 trials · 11,306 people`

### 2b. Taking it every day: duration (23–32 s)
- **Wording:** "In adults the duration of colds was reduced by 8% (3% to 12%) and in children by 14% (7% to 21%)." This is from 31 comparisons covering 9,745 cold episodes.
- **Our arithmetic:** 8% of a 7-day cold is 0.56 days, so "about half a day". The 7-day cold is an example, not a figure from the review.
- **On screen:** `adults −8% · children −14% (taken every day)`

### 2c. Taking it only once sick (32–41 s)
- **Wording:** "Seven comparisons examined the effect of therapeutic vitamin C (3249 episodes). No consistent effect of vitamin C was seen on the duration or severity of colds in the therapeutic trials."
- **Authors' nuance:** "it may be worthwhile for common cold patients to test on an individual basis whether therapeutic vitamin C is beneficial for them. Further therapeutic RCTs are warranted." Few therapeutic trials exist, so the film says "no consistent effect", not "doesn't work".
- **On screen:** `7 trials · no consistent effect`

### 2d. The exception: heavy physical stress (41–46 s)
- **Wording:** "Five trials involving a total of 598 marathon runners, skiers and soldiers on subarctic exercises yielded a pooled RR of 0.48 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.64)." Authors: "vitamin C may be useful for people exposed to brief periods of severe physical exercise."
- **Check:** "soldiers in the Arctic" becomes "soldiers on **subarctic** exercises". "Risk dropped by half" is RR 0.48, about half. The film says "about half" and "in 5 small trials".
- **On screen:** `5 trials · 598 people · RR 0.48`

## 3. Health wording
The narration uses "may" and "no consistent effect". It never says "useless", "proven" or "causes". "Two Nobel Prizes… and only half right" is the channel's verdict on the popular claim, not a quote.

## 4. Script claims (checked against the sections above)
- **Hook (chosen):** "Why do you still take vitamin C for colds? 29 trials, 11,306 people, and almost no fewer colds." 29 comparisons and 11,306 participants; RR 0.97 in the general community (2a).
- **"His 1970 book said big daily doses keep colds away"**: section 1.
- **"Taking it every day? About the same number of colds."**: 2a.
- **"8% in adults… in a seven-day cold, about half a day"**: 2b and our arithmetic.
- **"Many people take it only once they're sick"**: we found no source measuring how most people take it, so the script says "many", not "most".
- **"That version showed no consistent effect"**: 2c.
- **"marathon runners, skiers, soldiers on subarctic exercises… risk fell by about half"**: 2d (RR 0.48).
- **CTA:** A ("Follow, so we can keep checking what everyone believes."), the first in the rotation.
- **Closing line:** "A two-time Nobel winner, and only half right." It replaced "Two Nobel Prizes…" after the phone-speaker check misheard it. The meaning is the same.
