# Video 15: Why not just pour a concrete wall across the river?

Sources for the first *Why not just...?* Short on the *Actually, No* channel. Each claim is listed with its source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Sources read on 2026-11-07.

## 1. Water goes under the wall (seepage and piping)
- **Grout curtain definition:** US Army Corps of Engineers, *Gravity Dam Design*, EM 1110-2-2200 (30 June 1995), Glossary. "GROUT CURTAIN. A row of holes filled with grout under pressure near the heel of the dam to control seepage under the dam." Seepage under a dam is therefore a design problem the manual has a named fix for.
- **Uplift exists under every dam:** EM 1110-2-2200, para. 3-3d: "Uplift pressure resulting from headwater and tailwater exists through cross sections within the dam, at the interface between the dam and the foundation, and within the foundation below the base. This pressure is present within the cracks, pores, joints, and seams in the concrete and foundation material."
- **Piping (seepage that carries soil away):** Levy & Salvadori, *Why Buildings Fall Down* (1992), p. 162, define piping as "the development of tubular leak-causing cavities" (as quoted in the case study in section 4).
- **How common:** Von Thun J. L., "Understanding Piping and Seepage Failures: The No. 1 Dam Safety Problem in the West", ASDSO West Regional Conference, Lake Tahoe (1996), public abstract at damsafety.org: "for dams greater than 50 feet high piping is the predominant failure mode in the Western United States", and "60 percent of the dam failures in the Western United States for earth dams greater than 50 feet high were classified as piping failures." This figure applies only to that group of dams, so **the film does not show the 60%**.
- **Wording in the film:** the naive wall sits on river mud and gravel; water seeps under it and starts carrying soil out (drawn as a cartoon). No statistic is claimed.
- **On screen:** `USACE EM 1110-2-2200`

## 2. "Make it thicker" → water pushes up on the whole base (uplift)
- **Source:** EM 1110-2-2200, para. 3-3d: "Uplift pressure is an active force that must be included in the stability and stress analysis to ensure structural adequacy."
- **Acts on the whole base:** para. 3-3d(1)(a): "The uplift pressure will be considered as acting over 100 percent of the base." A thicker wall has a wider base, so the push from below acts over a bigger area.
- **Without drains:** para. 3-3d(1)(b): "the hydraulic gradient will be assumed to vary, as a straight line, from headwater at the heel to zero or tailwater at the toe."
- **Why it slides:** para. 4-6b(2) defines the sliding factor of safety as FS = (N tan φ + cL) / T, where "N = resultant of forces normal to the assumed sliding plane". Uplift pushes against the dam's weight and so reduces N, the force that holds the dam in place by friction.
- **Real case (not named in the film):** ASDSO Dam Failures, lesson "Concrete gravity dams should be evaluated to accommodate full uplift": "Large uplift pressures can compromise the structural adequacy of concrete gravity dams and serve as the principal cause of failure." On Austin (Bayless) Dam: "The dam's failure due to sliding can be directly attributable to the designer admittedly neglecting to account for uplift pressure."
- **Wording in the film:** "Water gets under it and pushes UP. The whole base gets lighter, and the wall slides." The comic exaggeration is in the drawing (the wall skidding), not in the claim.
- **On screen:** `USACE EM 1110-2-2200`

## 3. The real solution: rock, a grout curtain, and drains
- **Rock foundation:** EM 1110-2-2200, para. 1-3b: "The procedures in this manual cover only dams on rock foundations." Glossary: "BEDROCK. The solid rock foundation of a dam, usually overlain by soil or other unconsolidated superficial material." The film shows digging down through the soil to bedrock.
- **Grout curtain:** see section 1 (glossary). Para. 3-3d(1)(d): "For drainage to be controlled economically, retarding of flow to the drains from the upstream head is mandatory. This may be accomplished by a zone of grouting (curtain)". "Grout holes shall be oriented to intercept the maximum number of rock fractures".
- **Drains:** para. 3-3d(1)(c): "Uplift pressures at the base or below the foundation can be reduced by installing foundation drains." Their effectiveness "will be assumed to vary from 25 to 50 percent" in design (up to 67% with testing and approval). The grout zone "should be supplemented by foundation drain holes".
- **Together:** ASDSO (lesson above): "a grout curtain positioned upstream of a drain curtain within the dam's base can also facilitate the reduction of uplift pressures by eliminating or decreasing the number of seepage paths."
- **Shape:** the film draws the classic gravity-dam wedge (wide base, narrow top), the cross-section the manual designs.
- **Arch dams: NOT in the film.** The claim that an arch dam carries load "by arch action into the valley walls" is in USACE EM 1110-2-2201 (*Arch Dam Design*), but every copy we tried was blocked (HTTP 403), so we could not read it ourselves. The film leaves arch dams out.
- **Wording in the film:** "Dig to solid rock. Inject grout to seal the cracks. Then drill drains, so the water that still gets through can escape, instead of lifting the dam."
- **On screen:** `USACE EM 1110-2-2200` and the label words `GROUT` and `DRAINS`.

## 4. Teton Dam, Idaho, 1976
- **Official facts:** U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Teton Dam page (usbr.gov/pn/snakeriver/dams/uppersnake/teton/, last updated 4/13/26, read 2026-11-07): "On June 5, 1976, the Teton Dam in southeastern Idaho catastrophically failed." "This catastrophic failure resulted in the loss of 11 lives and caused millions in property damage to the surrounding communities."
- **Death toll conflict:** USBR says 11. The ASCE case study below says "14 killed directly or indirectly". The film uses the official **11**.
- **Cause, Independent Panel to Review Cause of Teton Dam Failure,** *Report to the U.S. Department of the Interior and State of Idaho on Failure of Teton Dam* (December 1976), pp. iii–iv. Quoted in the ASDSO abstract (damsafety.org) and in full in Solava S. & Delatte N., "Lessons from the Failure of the Teton Dam", 3rd ASCE Forensic Engineering Congress (2003), as summarized by S. Kramer (Univ. of Washington, CEE 521): "the dam failed by internal erosion (piping) of the core of the dam deep in the right foundation key trench", "openings existed through inadequately sealed rock joints", and "once started, piping progressed rapidly through the main body of the dam and quickly led to complete failure."
- **The grout curtain lesson (Panel, pp. vii–ix, via Solava & Delatte):** "too much was expected of the grout curtain, and … the design should have provided measures to render the inevitable leakage harmless." The panel also found the rock under the grout cap "was not adequately sealed".
- **Type and height:** an earthfill (zoned embankment) dam, 93 m (305 ft) high, failing during its first filling (Solava & Delatte 2003; GeoPrac, "50 Years Since the Teton Dam Failure", 2 Aug 2026: "The 305-foot-high earthfill dam failed during its first filling in 1976").
- **Important difference:** Teton was an **earth dam**, not a concrete wall. The film says so implicitly by drawing it as an earth embankment, and links it only to the shared lesson: water seeping through cracked rock under a dam, and a seal with no drains behind it.
- **Tone:** named respectfully, no flood imagery, no people drawn, no jokes in this scene.
- **Wording in the film:** "In 1976, Idaho's Teton Dam failed: water eroded a path through cracked rock under it. Eleven people died. Investigators said the dam needed a way to make the leakage harmless."
- **On screen:** `Independent Panel 1976 · USBR`
