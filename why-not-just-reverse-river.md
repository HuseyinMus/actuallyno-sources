# Video 21: Why not just move the drinking-water pipe? (Reversing the Chicago River)

Sources for this *Why not just...?* Short on the *Actually, No* channel. Each claim is listed with its source, the quoted wording, and the short credit shown on screen. Sources read on 2026-11-13.

Main sources:
- **Supreme Court 1906**: *Missouri v. Illinois*, 200 U.S. 496 (1906), opinion by Justice Holmes, via Cornell LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/200/496
- **Diversion history**: "A Short History of the Chicago Diversion" (International Upper Great Lakes Study context; citing the Sanitary District's own 1913 brief by L. E. Cooley): https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/cases/environment/ChicagoDiversionHistoryMarch5.pdf (read in full as text).
- **Lamb 1994**: John Lamb, "Chicago: The Hydraulic City and Its Environmental Impact", Illinois State Historical Society symposium paper, 2 Dec 1994: https://www.lewisu.edu/imcanal/johnlamb/section_44.pdf (read in full as text).
- **Capano 2003**: Daniel E. Capano, "Chicago's War With Water", *Invention & Technology*, Spring 2003, vol. 18 no. 4: https://www.inventionandtech.com/node/86364
- **MWRD**: Metropolitan Water Reclamation District of Greater Chicago (successor of the Sanitary District), "History": https://mwrd.org/history

## 1. The problem: sewage into the river, the river into the drinking water
- MWRD: "The city's first sewers were designed to flow straight to the Chicago River"; the District's job was "to take wastewater away from Lake Michigan… protecting the source of the region's drinking water."
- Capano 2003: the river "emptied into Lake Michigan, the city's main source of drinking water"; "Typhoid and cholera spread, their incidence spiking whenever heavy rains washed filth from the streets".
- **Wording in the film:** "Sewers ran into the river, the river ran into the lake, and the city drank the lake. That water was linked to typhoid and cholera." (health rule: "linked to")
- **On screen:** `MWRD · Capano, Invention & Technology 2003`

## 2. Fail 1: move the drinking-water intake farther out
- Diversion history: in 1885 the storm's effluent went "far out into Lake Michigan"; "winds from the northeast may have kept the sewage from reaching the city's water intakes two miles out."
- Capano 2003: "Plumes of contaminative sewage frequently overwhelmed the water intakes, particularly during rain."
- **Wording in the film:** "So, move the pipe? The intakes were already two miles out. In storms, the sewage plume followed."
- **On screen:** `Intakes ~2 miles out · Chicago Diversion history`

## 3. Fail 2: pump the river backwards
- Diversion history: "the Illinois and Michigan Canal was deepened and new pumps were added in 1871, effectively reversing the flow of the river under normal conditions."
- Capano 2003: "The new cut opened in July 1871"; flooding "caused the flow of the Chicago River to reverse again, pouring sewage and filth into Lake Michigan"; "In 1883 the Chicago government installed even bigger pumps"; in the August 1885 storm "The pumping facilities at Bridgeport were flooded and the river again reversed course".
- Diversion history: on August 2, 1885 a storm "dumped more than five inches of rain" in under 24 hours (Capano gives "more than six inches"; the film gives no rainfall number).
- **Wording in the film:** "Okay, then pump the river backwards! 1871: a deeper canal, bigger pumps." / "It worked, until a huge storm in 1885 flooded the pumps, and the river ran back."
- **On screen:** `1871 pumps · flooded in the 1885 storm (Capano 2003)`

## 4. The real solution: a canal deep enough that the lake drains itself
- Sanitary District: created in 1889 (MWRD: "formed as the Sanitary District of Chicago in 1889").
- Construction began in 1892 (Diversion history). Length: "28 miles to Lockport" (Lamb 1994); "ran parallel to the I&M for 28 miles" (Capano 2003).
- Depth: Lamb 1994: the canal's base was cut "26 feet below the floor of Lake Michigan"; Capano 2003: "it slopes downhill all the way". So lake water runs into it by gravity, carrying the river with it.
- Digging: Capano 2003: "workers moved more than 41 million cubic yards of dirt and rock".
- Opening: Capano 2003: "On the morning of January 2, 1900", workers broke a temporary dam and water "began to flow"; "It took 12 days to fill." The Supreme Court: "The Chicago drainage canal was opened on January 17, 1900." (The formal dedication was May 2, 1900, per the Diversion history.)
- Result: Capano 2003: "The Chicago River, now the first ever to flow away from its mouth, purged itself". MWRD: "The MWRD reversed the Chicago River in 1900 by constructing the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal".
- **Wording in the film:** "a canal 28 miles long, cut deeper than the lake bed." / "Now the lake drains downhill into it, dragging the river along. January 1900: they broke the last dam."
- **On screen:** `28 miles, opened Jan 1900 · Lamb 1994 · Capano 2003`

## 5. The twist: the state downstream sued, and lost
- Supreme Court 1906: Missouri sued "to restrain the discharge of the sewage of Chicago through an artificial channel into the Desplaines river", claiming it would "send 1,500 tons of poisonous filth daily into the Mississippi". The Court: "The plaintiff's case depends upon an inference of the unseen." "Bill dismissed without prejudice." Decided February 19, 1906.
- **Wording in the film:** "Missouri, downstream, sued Chicago at the Supreme Court. In 1906, the case was thrown out." (The case was Missouri v. Illinois and the Sanitary District of Chicago; "Bill dismissed without prejudice.")
- **On screen:** `Missouri v. Illinois, 200 U.S. 496 (1906)`

## Checked and dropped
- "90,000 people died in 1885": the Diversion history calls this an erroneous modern claim ("records show a death rate from typhoid only slightly above average… and no deaths from cholera"). Not used, and the 1885 storm is not linked to deaths in the film.
- Rainfall in 1885 (five vs. six inches): sources differ; no number in the film.
- "The river still flows backwards today", the ASCE "Monument of the Millennium" (1999): not found in a readable source; not used.
